What a feed ranker learns from matters more than the model.

A short-video app replaced some recommendations with videos drawn at random and released the logs. Users' responses to those videos show what a ranker would obtain, with no model of how the feed chooses. Change how long a list the ranker shows, which offline score picks the model, and what it is rewarded for, and the charts follow.

1 · Which logs to learn from

Trained on random exposures, the ranker's picks are long-viewed far more often, at any list length.

Each user's random exposures are ranked by a model, and the top of the list is what a feed would show. The gap between training logs dwarfs the gap between models.

2 · How the ranker is scored

The usual offline check picks the ranker that recommends worse.

The model trained on the standard feed wins on held-out standard-feed exposures. Scored on exposures the feed did not choose, the order reverses.

Which ranker wins depends on what it is scored on

Choose a score. The badge marks the ranker a team would ship on that score.

Both rankers are the same network; only the training logs differ. Within-user AUC is the share of pairs of the same user's exposures, one long-viewed and one not, that a score orders correctly.

3 · What it is rewarded for

Rewarded for the share of a video watched, the ranker picks 9-second videos.

The same network trained on three labels. A reward that can be met by picking short videos is met that way.

4 · A new video, scored from its caption

A language model's opinion of a caption is close to chance.

For videos no model has seen viewed, a tree model on length, category and upload type ranks a user's candidates; a 7-billion-parameter language model asked about the caption does not.

Within-user AUC on the test videos' random exposures

Bars are 95% intervals from 500 resamples of test videos. The dashed line is chance (0.5). The reference uses the video's own long-view rate among its other viewers, which only exists after the video has been shown.
